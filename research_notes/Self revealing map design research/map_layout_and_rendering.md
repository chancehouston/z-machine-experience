# Map layout and rendering for a growing graph of places on a phone screen

Reading notes: "Cited Findings" are things confirmed from a source opened or returned by search in this session. Most were confirmed from abstracts, repository listings or search excerpts rather than full texts; where that matters it is said. "Inferences" are my own reasoning. Items I know from background knowledge but did not verify in this session are placed under "Gaps" and marked "unverified" so the report writer does not treat them as sourced.

## Layout: how are graphs with direction-labelled edges laid out so that north is up and lines stay straight?

### Takeaway
Three families exist: (1) lattice placement, where each exit is a unit step on an integer grid and collisions are resolved by stretching or bending (what text-adventure and MUD tools do); (2) optimisation over an octilinear model, as in metro maps (high quality, but mixed-integer programming or hill climbing, not millisecond-scale by default); (3) stress or force layout with axis-aligned separation and alignment constraints (IPSep-CoLa, WebCola), which expresses "B is north of A" directly as a constraint. No tool found handles a contradictory set of directions automatically and well; existing tools either fall back to manual editing, bent connection lines, or leave overlapping rooms.

### Cited Findings

Lattice and text-adventure / MUD tools
- IFM (Interactive Fiction Mapper) places rooms on a grid; links leave rooms in any of the eight standard compass directions and also follow the grid — [IFM documentation, "Making maps"](https://ifm.readthedocs.io/en/latest/maps.html)
- Inform 7's own index map ("Spatial Map" in its index module) treats connections in lattice directions as proximity between rooms on a lattice (search excerpt only; the source page was blocked to automated fetch) — [Inform 7 Spatial Map source mirror](https://code.oreolek.me/ifhub/inform7/src/commit/07f58e4006edc1da8c3b920029d2fae1dcf592d0/inter/index-module/Chapter%204/Spatial%20Map.w)
- Mudlet shows a room only once it has a unique integer id, an area id and integer x, y, z coordinates; a room belongs to exactly one area ("rooms cannot be in more than 1 area") — [Mudlet Manual: Mapper](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper)
- Mudlet rooms are all the same size and coordinates are integers marking the room centre, so a script that wants varying room sizes must handle that itself — [Mudlet forum](https://forums.mudlet.org/viewtopic.php?p=15062)
- Mudlet treats up, down, in and out as ordinary numbered directions (9 to 12) alongside the compass ones, and draws them as symbols on the room rather than as lines; "stub" exits are drawn for exits whose destination is not yet known ("draw exit directions even though the exit rooms have not yet been defined"), with a different symbol fill for stub versus known up/down/in/out exits — [Mudlet Manual: Mapper](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper)
- Mudlet has "custom exit lines": user-drawn poly-lines for exits that do not fit the grid; they "are purely visual tools and have no effect on the pathfinding" — [Mudlet Manual: Mapper](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper)
- Mudlet's manual tools for a crowded map: spread or shrink a selection of rooms by a multiplication factor ("2 is a good number to try") — [Mudlet Manual: Mapper](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper)
- Mudlet's bundled generic mapper "tries to draw a map by guessing most of the information from your game"; its map-save step merges rooms that share both coordinates and a room name — [Mudlet Manual: Mapper](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper); [Generic mapper package](https://cnwiki.mudlet.org/w/Generic_mapper_package)

What happens when directions cannot all be honoured
- Mudlet users auto-mapping without server-supplied coordinates report many "collisions", one room trying to take coordinates already held by another; a forum reply states that a fully automated mapper needs either game layouts that never overlap or a more complicated script that dynamically expands the map — [Mudlet forum thread](https://forums.mudlet.org/viewtopic.php?p=12946); [Mudlet forum thread](https://forums.mudlet.org/viewtopic.php?p=14976)
- Trizbort's automapper is described by its own maintainer as an area that "could use some attention"; it relies on distinct room names and "doesn't handle mazes quite as nicely" — [intfiction.org: Trizbort and Euclidean Mazes](https://intfiction.org/t/trizbort-and-euclidean-mazes/14134)
- For a game whose connections are bent (going north from A reaches B, but the way back is not south), a user reports Trizbort's automapping is "pretty much useless"; the suggested remedy is drawing connection lines with a 90-degree bend by hand — [intfiction.org: Christminster Directional Confusion](https://intfiction.org/t/christminster-directional-confusion/41016)
- A separate forum thread exists on automapping with non-opposite directions in Inform 7, confirming this is a recognised recurring problem — [intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/i7-automapping-with-non-opposite-directions/3426)

Octilinear (metro map) layout
- Nöllenburg and Wolff identify seven design rules used in most real metro maps, split them into hard and soft constraints, and encode them as a mixed-integer program that finds a drawing satisfying all hard constraints (if one exists) while minimising a weighted sum of soft-constraint costs (bends, positions, lengths); layout and station labelling are solved together. Published IEEE TVCG 17(5):626–641, 2011; preliminary version at Graph Drawing 2005 — [Nöllenburg and Wolff, paper PDF](https://i11www.iti.kit.edu/extra/publications/nw-dlhqm-10.pdf); [2006 precursor PDF](https://i11www.iti.kit.edu/extra/publications/nw-amipd-06.pdf)
- In the original model each edge is given a range of three admissible directions: the octilinear direction nearest its input direction plus the two neighbouring ones — [Nickel and Nöllenburg, "Towards Data-Driven Multilinear Metro Maps"](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1904.03039)
- Stott and Rodgers use a hill-climbing optimiser whose fitness is a weighted sum of several aesthetic metrics, starting from the geographic layout or a sketch; the journal version (TVCG 2011, with Martinez-Ovando and Walker) adds clustering so the climber moves groups of stations as well as single stations, to escape local minima — [Stott and Rodgers 2004 PDF](https://www.cs.kent.ac.uk/pubs/2004/1925/content.pdf); [Stott et al. 2011 PDF](https://www.cs.kent.ac.uk/pubs/2011/2963/content.pdf)
- A later survey contrasts metro-map methods that keep vertex positions near their originals (to preserve the mental map) with those that maximise aesthetics such as symmetry — [Misue/Takahashi preprint](https://www.u-aizu.ac.jp/~shigeo/research/mental/ijacdt2019-preprint.pdf)

Constraint-based and force-directed layout with direction constraints
- IPSep-CoLa (Dwyer, Koren, Marriott; IEEE TVCG 12(5):821–828, 2006) adds separation constraints (a minimum horizontal or vertical gap between chosen node pairs) to stress majorization; the result is a quadratic programme solved by an incremental gradient-projection method, reported as much faster than generic constraint solvers and comparable in speed to unconstrained stress majorization. It is limited to linear, axis-aligned constraints, and the constraints must be satisfiable — [paper summary](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/ipsep-cola-an-incremental-procedure-for-separation-constraint-layout-of-graphs/812038880860569601-2445); [Dwyer slides](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/courses/533-09/slides/dwyer.subset.pdf)
- Graphviz ships an implementation (`constrained_majorization_ipsep.c`) with constraint types for directed edges, node non-overlap and cluster containment — [Graphviz source docs](https://www2.graphviz.org/Archive/development/doxygen/html/constrained__majorization__ipsep_8c_source.html)
- WebCola supports three kinds of constraint: separation (minimum gap between two nodes on one axis, or an exact gap with `equality: true`), alignment (nodes share an x or y, with offsets), and groups (nodes kept inside a padded rectangle) — [WebCola wiki: Constraints](https://github.com/tgdwyer/WebCola/wiki/Constraints)
- Follow-up work by Dwyer and Marriott improves convergence by diagonally scaling the stress function, and Dwyer, Marriott and Wybrow integrate edge routing into the same framework — [GD 2007 paper](https://rd.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-77537-9_23)

### Inferences
- The direction vocabulary maps cleanly onto WebCola-style constraints: "B is east of A" is an alignment constraint on y plus a separation constraint on x (B.x − A.x ≥ gap); a diagonal is two separation constraints with equal gaps. Because these are inequality constraints, a long corridor can stretch to make room without bending. This is the principled version of what Mudlet users do by hand with "spread by factor 2".
- IPSep-CoLa's requirement that constraints be satisfiable is exactly the failure case of adventure maps (loops that do not close geometrically, one-way and twisting passages, mazes). A practical design therefore needs a tiered policy: (a) honour every exit as a hard constraint while consistent; (b) on contradiction, detect the cycle whose displacement vectors do not sum to zero and demote the newest or least-used edge to a "soft" edge; (c) draw demoted edges differently (curved or dog-legged line with the true exit direction shown as a stub at each end), the automated counterpart of Mudlet's custom exit lines and Trizbort's hand-drawn 90-degree bends.
- For a few hundred rooms an integer-lattice approach is the cheapest route to the stated budget: place the new room at parent + unit vector; if occupied, try inserting an empty row or column (a "stretch" that shifts everything beyond a cut line, which preserves all relative orderings); if the room is the same room reached by another route, the edge is the non-closing one and gets demoted. Each insertion is linear in the number of rooms, comfortably within milliseconds in Rust.
- The metro-map MIP is too heavy for per-move layout on a phone, but its rule list (hard: octilinearity, preserved topology, minimum edge length; soft: few bends, short edges, relative position) is a good cost function for an occasional background "tidy" pass, and Stott-style hill climbing started from the current layout is naturally incremental (small moves from the existing state).
- The "three admissible directions" relaxation from Nöllenburg and Wolff suggests a middle tier for conflicts: allow an edge to deviate by 45 degrees before demoting it to a fully bent connector.

### Gaps
- Inform 7's spatial-map algorithm is documented in detail in its literate source, but both mirrors refused automated fetches. Unverified from memory: it positions rooms on a 3D lattice, divides the map into components, and uses a cost ("heat") function with tactics for moving sub-maps apart when rooms collide, and "locks" exits that it will not bend. This should be read directly at the Inform GitHub repository before relying on it; it is the closest prior art to the problem.
- Trizbort's automap placement code was not examined; no documentation of its algorithm was found. The source (GitHub, C#) would need reading.
- No published benchmark was found for constrained stress layout at a few hundred nodes on a phone-class processor. Unverified expectation: well within budget for an incremental update with warm start, but this needs measuring.
- No Rust port of WebCola/libcola was confirmed in this session. Unverified: the Adaptagrams C++ libraries (libcola, libavoid) exist and could be linked, and stress majorization is simple to write natively.
- Orthogonal layout in the classic graph-drawing sense (topology-shape-metrics, Tamassia's bend minimisation) and the "Handbook of Graph Drawing and Visualization" chapters were not consulted in this session. Unverified: those methods choose edge directions themselves, so they do not directly apply when the direction of every edge is given; the given-direction problem is closer to "drawing with prescribed edge directions" and to metro maps.

## Stability: how is a layout kept steady as nodes are added, and what does the research say about tolerated change and animation?

### Takeaway
The evidence that strict mental-map preservation improves task performance is weaker than commonly assumed: Archambault and Purchase found little or no effect for general comprehension and memorability tasks, but a significant benefit for orientation tasks (finding and re-finding specific places), which is the task a player's map serves. Animated transitions help people track which objects moved; small multiples are faster for general reading but less accurate for spotting what was added.

### Cited Findings
- Archambault, Purchase and Pinaud (IEEE TVCG 2011): small multiples gave faster responses than animation overall and on each of five tasks; animation gave fewer errors on tasks asking which nodes or edges appeared in the same time slice; mental map preservation had little influence on error rate or response time in either presentation — [Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/animation-small-multiples-and-the-effect-of-mental-map-preservati/); [Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/66915)
- Archambault and Purchase (PacificVis 2012, memorability): no significant difference in response time or error rate from preserving the mental map, though qualitative comments suggested preservation made the tasks feel easier — [Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/78675)
- Archambault and Purchase (Graph Drawing 2012, "Mental Map Preservation Helps User Orientation in Dynamic Graphs"): for orientation tasks, preserving the mental map was significantly faster and less error-prone, and the benefit held as the number of targets grew — [Springer](https://rd.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-36763-2_42)
- Their overview (IJHCS 2013, "The 'Map' in the mental map") concluded that no experiment to that date had found conclusive evidence for the mental map's effectiveness in comprehending a dynamic graph series — [Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/the-map-in-the-mental-map-experimental-results-in-dynamic-graph-d/)
- Heer and Robertson (InfoVis 2007): two controlled experiments comparing animated with static transitions found significant advantages for animation in object tracking and value estimation; the paper gives a taxonomy of transitions and design guidelines — [paper PDF](https://vis.berkeley.edu/papers/animated_transitions/2007-AnimatedTransitions-InfoVis.pdf); [UW IDL page](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/animated-transitions)
- Metro-map literature frames the same trade-off as two camps: keep vertex positions largely fixed to maintain the mental map, or re-optimise for aesthetics — [Misue/Takahashi preprint](https://www.u-aizu.ac.jp/~shigeo/research/mental/ijacdt2019-preprint.pdf)

### Inferences
- A player uses the map to orient ("where am I, where was the cellar"), not to analyse graph structure, so the one positive result (orientation) is the relevant one. Design rule: never move a room the player has already seen unless forced, and when forced, move the smallest possible set.
- The cheapest strong guarantee is to preserve orthogonal ordering: if A was left of and above B before an update, it stays so after. Row or column insertion on a lattice preserves this by construction; free re-optimisation with stress does not unless old positions are pinned or given heavy anchor weights.
- With a constraint solver, stability is obtained by warm-starting from the previous positions and adding a penalty term pulling each old node to its old position; only the new node and nodes implicated in a violated constraint are free.
- When rooms must move, animate the move (Heer and Robertson: animation helps tracking), keep it short and in one stage for a stretch (all moved rooms translate by the same vector, which reads as one rigid motion), and add the new room only after the stretch finishes so that two kinds of change are not shown at once. With Reduce Motion on, replace with a cross-fade (see the accessibility section).
- A periodic global "tidy" that would rearrange many rooms should be an explicit user action, not automatic.

### Gaps
- No quantitative threshold for "how much change people tolerate" was found; the Archambault and Purchase studies compare preserved against not-preserved conditions rather than measuring a tolerance curve. I found no reliable source giving one.
- The Beck, Burch, Diehl and Weiskopf survey of dynamic graph visualisation was not retrieved. Unverified: it classifies techniques as animation versus timeline and discusses online (incremental) layout, where future states are unknown, as a distinct and harder problem; this map is an online problem.
- The original formal definitions of mental-map preservation (Misue, Eades, Lai and Sugiyama 1995: orthogonal ordering, proximity relations, topology) were not retrieved; the orthogonal-ordering idea above rests on unverified memory of that paper.
- Unverified: studies of staged animation and of animation duration (commonly cited guidance is roughly a quarter to one second, slow-in slow-out) were not checked this session.

## Levels and regions: multi-floor spaces on a flat screen, and grouping large graphs across zoom levels

### Takeaway
The established pattern for floors is one flat plan per level with a level picker (Apple's indoor maps, Mudlet's z-levels), with vertical connections drawn as symbols on the room rather than as lines. For scale, the HCI literature distinguishes overview-plus-detail (minimap), zooming (including semantic zoom) and focus-plus-context (fisheye); regions map naturally onto Mudlet-style "areas" and WebCola-style group rectangles.

### Cited Findings
- Apple's indoor maps use the Indoor Mapping Data Format (GeoJSON); each level has a full name (for example "parking level one"), a short name ("P1") and a reference to its building; the WWDC 2019 session shows a level picker at the top right for switching floors, and Apple's sample notes developers supply their own colours, icons and level picker — [WWDC 2019 session 241](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/241); [Apple: Displaying an Indoor Map](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/mapkit/displaying-an-indoor-map)
- A third-party tutorial on the same material uses a segmented control to change level, redrawing the overlays for the selected level — [Kodeco: Indoor Maps on iOS](https://www.raywenderlich.com/12690970-indoor-maps-on-ios-advanced-mapkit/page/3)
- Mudlet gives every room a z coordinate, shows one z-level at a time (selection can cover "the current z-level" or "all z-levels"), and marks up/down/in/out exits with symbols on the room square — [Mudlet Manual: Mapper](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper)
- Mudlet partitions the world into "areas"; each area is a separate map and a room is in exactly one — [Mudlet Manual: Mapper](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper)
- WebCola group constraints keep a set of nodes inside a padded rectangle, which gives non-overlapping region boxes as part of the layout — [WebCola wiki: Constraints](https://github.com/tgdwyer/WebCola/wiki/Constraints)
- Cockburn, Karlson and Bederson (ACM Computing Surveys 41(1), 2008) classify the approaches: overview+detail separates the two views in space; zooming separates them in time; focus+context (fisheye) merges them in one distorted view; cue-based techniques highlight or suppress items in place. The review covers semantic zooming and ends with guidelines — [Microsoft Research PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2008/01/cockburn-ComputingSurveys09.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Up" and "down" should change level rather than consume screen directions; a level picker (stepper showing level names, where the name can be derived from the first room or a region name) plus stair symbols on rooms follows both Apple's and Mudlet's practice. A faint "ghost" of the level below, drawn in a lighter pencil weight, gives vertical context cheaply and suits the paper style.
- "In" and "out" have no canonical geometry. Options: treat as a level change into a nested sub-map (an inset or separate region), or place by a default vector and mark as a soft edge. Mudlet's choice of a symbol on the room is the lowest-risk default.
- Semantic zoom tiers suited to this graph: far (regions as labelled blobs, only inter-region links), middle (rooms as boxes without labels, current room and route highlighted), near (room names, exit stubs, item marks). On a phone a separate minimap costs scarce space; a zoom-out gesture or a transient overview is the lower-cost equivalent of overview+detail.
- Fisheye distortion would bend the straight compass lines that the layout works to keep, undermining "north is up, lines stay straight"; it is a poor fit here.
- Region grouping can be computed from the graph (areas the game names, or cut points such as a single door between two clusters) and kept stable by assigning a room to a region once.

### Gaps
- The empirical conclusions of the Cockburn, Karlson and Bederson review were not extracted (only taxonomy confirmed). Unverified from memory: the review reports mixed results for fisheye views, with distortion harming targeting and spatial memory in several studies, and generally favourable results for overview+detail; this should be checked in the PDF before being cited.
- Google Maps' indoor level switcher was not sourced this session. Unverified: it shows a vertical floor selector at the screen edge when zoomed into a mapped building.
- No sources were retrieved on exploded isometric or stacked-layer ("2.5D") views for multi-floor plans, nor on evidence comparing them with a level switcher. I found no reliable source on this within the session.

## Rendering style: hand-drawn and pencil-on-paper looks by program, generated fantasy-style maps, and what is feasible on iOS

### Takeaway
The hand-drawn look is a small, well-understood set of tricks: perturb endpoints and bow lines, draw each stroke twice, fill with hatching generated by scan lines, and seed the randomness so a shape looks the same every frame. Rough.js is the reference implementation, a Rust port exists, and dungeon-map "Dyson" hatching has a public write-up from Red Blob Games. SwiftUI Canvas (immediate mode) is sufficient for a few hundred rooms.

### Cited Findings
- Rough.js is a small library that draws in a sketchy, hand-drawn-like style on Canvas and SVG; options such as `roughness` and `bowing` control irregularity and curvature; a `seed` option makes the randomness repeatable so a shape keeps the same outline between renders; fills include hachure, cross-hatch, zigzag and others built on hachure lines — [roughjs.com](https://roughjs.com/); [Rough.js repository](https://github.com/rough-stuff/rough)
- Rough.js states that some core algorithms were adapted from the "handy" Processing library, and that its newer hachure fill is based on scan-line rasterisation; the algorithm auto-adjusts roughness to the size of the shape — [Rough.js repository](https://github.com/rough-stuff/rough)
- A Rust port of Rough.js exists as the `roughr` crate (version 0.12.0 seen) — [docs.rs: roughr](https://docs.rs/crate/roughr/latest/source/src/lib.rs)
- draw.io adopted Rough.js for a hand-drawn diagram style, evidence that the approach works for node-link diagrams — [draw.io blog](https://www2.drawio.com/blog/rough-style)
- Watabou's One Page Dungeon generator draws cross-hatching around rooms in the style of mapmaker Dyson Logos; the author described the method in a Patreon post and says it has been improved since while the core idea remains — [Watabou itch.io comments](https://watabou.itch.io/one-page-dungeon/comments)
- Red Blob Games re-implemented this "Dyson hatching" (21 Nov 2019): seed points are scattered, each cell gets a small group of parallel strokes at its own angle, and hatching is kept off rooms either by choosing seeds near walls and clipping, or by using a distance field so hatching fades away from walls — [Red Blob Games: Dyson Hatching](https://redblobgames.com/x/1947-dyson-hatching)
- SwiftUI `Canvas` is an immediate-mode drawing view intended for high-performance drawing of many elements; its closure is not a view builder, and SwiftUI views can be drawn into it only via tagged `symbols`; `TimelineView(.animation)` drives per-frame redraws, and everything inside the TimelineView closure is re-rendered each tick, so static content should be kept outside it — [Kodeco: Using TimelineView and Canvas](https://www.kodeco.com/27594491-using-timelineview-and-canvas-in-swiftui?page=1); [Swift with Majid: Mastering Canvas](https://swiftwithmajid.com/2023/04/11/mastering-canvas-in-swiftui/)
- Canvas has no per-element accessibility tree; accessibility information applies to the canvas as a whole unless a parallel hierarchy is supplied with `accessibilityChildren` — [notes on WWDC21 "Add rich graphics to your SwiftUI app"](https://mackuba.eu/notes/wwdc21/add-rich-graphics-to-swiftui-app)

### Inferences
- Division of labour that fits the architecture: the Rust library computes layout and also the stroke geometry (via `roughr` or a few hundred lines of own code: jittered double strokes, bowed lines, hachure), seeded by room id so each room's wobble is permanent; Swift receives poly-lines or path commands and draws them in Canvas. Seeding by id matters for stability: a room whose outline re-randomises each frame "boils", which is distracting and conflicts with the mental-map goal.
- Paper and pencil texture: a tiled paper image under the Canvas, strokes drawn with slightly varying opacity and width, and a multiply blend give most of the effect without shaders. Graphite grain along strokes is the first thing that would call for Metal or a Core Image filter; it is optional.
- Frame budget: a few hundred rooms at roughly ten to twenty short strokes each is a few thousand path segments, comfortably within immediate-mode Canvas. Static layers (paper, settled rooms) can be rasterised once and redrawn only on change; only the current-room marker and in-flight animations need per-frame drawing.
- A "self-revealing" map suits the style: new rooms can be "drawn in" by animating stroke length (trim the path from 0 to 1), which is cheap and reads as a pencil sketching; unexplored exits as short faded stubs follow Mudlet's stub convention.
- SpriteKit, SceneKit, RealityKit and Metal are unnecessary for the 2D case; they become relevant only for a 3D or headset view (next section).

### Gaps
- Wood, Isenberg, Isenberg, Dykes, Boukhelifa and Slingsby, "Sketchy Rendering for Information Visualization" (IEEE TVCG 2012) was not retrieved; search did not confirm its link to Rough.js. Unverified from memory: it introduced the Handy renderer for Processing (the library Rough.js credits) and reported experiments on how sketchiness affects perception of area and engagement. Should be checked before citing.
- The exact Rough.js line algorithm (endpoint offsets, two-pass stroke, mid-point bowing) was not confirmed from a primary write-up this session; the author's "How does Rough.js work" article was not fetched.
- No sources retrieved for Dungeon Scrawl or Inkarnate rendering techniques, nor for Red Blob Games' "noisy edges" in polygon map generation. Unverified: Inkarnate is a stamp-and-brush raster editor with hand-painted assets, not procedural line work; Dungeon Scrawl offers selectable hatching and line styles in the browser.
- Apple's primary WWDC21 session page for Canvas was not fetched (secondary notes used). No measured Canvas performance figures for path counts were found.
- No source was found comparing Canvas, Core Graphics, SpriteKit and Metal for this kind of line drawing on current iPhones.

## 3D: what is known about 3D or point-cloud maps for navigating abstract spaces

### Takeaway
3D helps graph reading only with real depth cues: stereo and motion parallax together let people trace paths in graphs roughly an order of magnitude larger than in 2D. On a flat, hand-held screen without those cues the evidence points the other way: Cockburn and McKenzie found that a third dimension did not help spatial memory and that performance and subjective ratings worsened as depth freedom increased.

### Cited Findings
- Ware and Franck (ACM TOG 1996): structured 3D motion and stereo viewing both help in understanding information nets; the kind of motion matters little — [UNH Scholars record](https://scholars.unh.edu/ccom/939)
- Ware and Mitchell (APGV 2005): on a high-resolution stereoscopic display with both stereo and motion cues, unskilled observers could see paths between nodes in 333-node graphs with better than 10 percent error — [UNH Scholars record](https://scholars.unh.edu/ccom/355)
- Ware and Mitchell (ACM TAP 2008): skilled observers could read graphs of up to 1000 nodes with under 10 percent error, described as an order-of-magnitude increase over 2D display — [UNH Scholars record](https://scholars.unh.edu/ccom/467)
- Cockburn and McKenzie (CHI 2002, pp. 203–210): in both physical and virtual versions of an item-retrieval task, performance deteriorated as freedom to place items in the third dimension increased; participants rated the higher-dimensional interfaces as more cluttered and less efficient — [DOI record](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/503376.503413); [paper PDF](https://courses.washington.edu/hcde511/a14/readings/cockburn-chi02.pdf)
- Cockburn (2004 extended study, "Evaluating spatial memory in two and three dimensions"): no significant difference in the effectiveness of spatial memory between 2D and 3D computer interfaces; with physical models, 2D gave better recall than 3D; the abstract concludes that adding a third dimension to computer displays does not aid spatial memory — [paper record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/evaluating-spatial-memory-in-two-and-three-dimensions/812365478621085697-2519)

### Inferences
- Ware's results depend on stereo plus head- or object-motion on high-resolution displays and concern path tracing in dense graphs. A phone has neither stereo nor head-coupled parallax; a rotating 3D point cloud on a phone gets only object-motion cues, and costs occlusion, foreshortened labels and loss of the "north is up" frame. A headset supplies the cues Ware relied on, so a 3D map is defensible there as a secondary mode.
- The player's graph is sparse and nearly planar per level (a few hundred nodes, degree at most twelve), far below the density where 3D paid off in Ware's experiments. The 2D layout with a level picker is the evidence-backed default; a 3D or exploded view is best treated as an optional flourish for showing how floors stack, not as the working map.
- If a 3D view is offered, constrain it: fixed "north is away" orientation, limited tilt, levels as stacked flat sheets (2.5D) so each sheet remains a readable 2D map.

### Gaps
- No studies were found in this session that specifically test 3D or point-cloud maps of abstract (non-geographic) spaces on phones, or node-link reading in consumer headsets. Unverified: immersive-analytics work (for example Kwon et al. on spherical graph layouts in VR; Cordeil et al.) reports benefits for graph tasks in head-mounted displays, but this was not checked.
- The figures in the Cockburn and McKenzie paper (retrieval times) were seen only as fragments from an incomplete copy and are not reported here.
- No evidence was gathered on motion sickness or fatigue for hand-held 3D map manipulation.

## Interaction on a phone: pan, pinch, tap-to-travel, details, reach, and sharing the screen with text and a keyboard

### Takeaway
Tap-to-travel over the known graph is a solved pattern in MUD clients: A* over explored exits with weights and locks, emitting a sequence of direction commands. Guidance specific to map-plus-text-feed layouts on phones was not found in the literature searched; the recommendations here are mostly inference.

### Cited Findings
- Mudlet: "Path finding uses the fast A* search algorithm"; it chooses the path with the fastest travel time using room weights rather than the fewest steps; exit weights were added later; rooms and exits can be locked, which removes them from the speed-walk graph — [Mudlet Manual: Mapper](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper)
- Mudlet's click-to-walk: clicking a room on the map calls a script hook (`doSpeedWalk()`), which receives the route both as a list of directions (`speedWalkDir`) and of room ids (`speedWalkPath`), and sends the moves; a script can opt to receive only the start and goal and do its own path-finding — [Mudlet Manual: Mapper](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper)
- Overview+detail with linked navigation lets a user explore the overview without changing the detail view — [Munzner course slides summarising Cockburn et al.](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/courses/533-09/slides/nav.pdf)

### Inferences
- Path-finding over a few hundred rooms is microseconds in Rust (breadth-first search suffices when all steps cost the same; A* or Dijkstra with weights if some exits should be avoided). Exits should be "lockable" automatically: one-way exits, exits that previously failed, exits needing an object, and exits where the game's response was not a simple move, so that tap-to-travel never walks the player into a state change they did not expect.
- Travel should be interruptible and visible: draw the planned route on the map, send one move per turn, stop if the game's reply does not land in the expected room (the map cannot know about locked doors, darkness or a wandering thief until it tries).
- Gesture allocation that avoids conflicts: one-finger drag pans, pinch zooms, tap on a room selects and offers "go here", tap on an exit stub of the current room moves one step, long-press opens details (name, description as first seen, items noted). A "recentre on me" control is needed because free panning loses the current room.
- One-handed reach: primary controls (recentre, level picker, toggle between map and text) belong in the lower third; the top of the screen is for passive information (region name).
- Sharing with text and keyboard: when the keyboard is up, roughly half the screen is gone; the map should then shrink to a strip showing the current room and its immediate neighbours (a local "compass rose" view), expanding to full size when the keyboard is dismissed. A draggable split or sheet with detents (map peek, half, full) is the native iOS idiom for two panes on a small screen. Auto-follow should keep the current room in view within the visible, unobscured region.
- Tapping an exit stub or neighbour as a way to issue "go north" makes the map an input device and reduces keyboard time, which is a strong reason for making it the main screen.

### Gaps
- No sourced examples were gathered of apps where a map and a text feed share a small screen well. Unverified candidates worth examining directly: MUD clients on iOS and Android with mini-maps; ride-hailing and delivery apps (map above, bottom sheet with detents below); Apple Maps' own bottom sheet; the Frotz and Lectrote interpreters (no map); the game "80 Days" and Inkle's "Sorcery!" (text driven by a map). None was verified in this session.
- Apple's Human Interface Guidelines on sheets, detents, gestures and minimum tap-target size were not fetched. Unverified: the commonly cited minimum target is 44 by 44 points, which constrains how small room boxes can be at the default zoom and therefore how many rooms fit on screen (roughly a 7 by 12 grid of bare targets on a typical phone before any spacing).
- No research on thumb-reach zones was retrieved (unverified: Hoober's observational studies of how people hold phones).

## Accessibility: screen readers, Dynamic Type and reduced motion

### Takeaway
A drawn map is invisible to VoiceOver unless a parallel structure is supplied. Research on accessible node-link diagrams converges on: give an overview first, make each node a focusable element that speaks its name and its connections, and let navigation follow the diagram's own structure (here, the compass exits). Apple's audio-graph API targets data series and is not a natural fit for a room graph.

### Cited Findings
- SwiftUI Canvas exposes no individual elements to accessibility; `accessibilityChildren` supplies a separate SwiftUI hierarchy used only for accessibility, describing the drawing's parts — [notes on WWDC21 session](https://mackuba.eu/notes/wwdc21/add-rich-graphics-to-swiftui-app); [Swift with Majid](https://swiftwithmajid.com/2023/04/11/mastering-canvas-in-swiftui/)
- Apple's audio graphs: a view supplies an `AXChartDescriptor` (in SwiftUI via `AXChartDescriptorRepresentable` and `makeChartDescriptor()`), which holds the chart's semantic information (title, summary, axes, data series); VoiceOver then offers an Audio Graph option in the rotor, with an interactive mode where the user double-taps, holds and drags to hear the data; it can be attached to any view — [Apple: Audio graphs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/accessibility/audio-graphs); [Apple: AXChartDescriptorRepresentable](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/axchartdescriptorrepresentable); [Swift with Majid: Audio graphs in SwiftUI](https://swiftwithmajid.com/2021/09/29/audio-graphs-in-swiftui/); [WWDC 2021 session 10122](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10122)
- Benthic (Mei et al., ASSETS 2025) argues for "perceptually congruent" screen-reader structures: navigation should mirror the graphic's visual structure; it models graphics as hypergraphs, covers diagrams and maps as well as charts, and notes that earlier tools capture only part of the structure (Olli and Chart Reader use trees; Data Navigator uses node-link graphs) — [MIT Visualization Group: Benthic](https://vis.csail.mit.edu/pubs/benthic)
- TADA (Zhao et al., CHI 2024): a tablet system making node-link diagrams accessible to blind and low-vision people with touch exploration, musical tones and speech; supports overview, navigation, detail, filtering and finding; evaluated with 25 participants — [arXiv 2311.04502](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.04502v4)
- Balik et al.'s Graph Sketching tool: keyboard navigation of node-link graphs where each time a node or edge receives focus its information is voiced by the screen reader — [Balik et al. 2014 PDF](https://ciigar.csc.ncsu.edu/files/bib/Balik2014-AccessGraphs.pdf)
- A 2022 study of blind users and digital whiteboards (Fan, Glazko, Follmer) recommends reflecting diagram structure in the navigation scheme and providing a spatial overview — cited in [TADA, arXiv 2311.04502](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.04502v4)
- This project already speaks each game reply to a screen reader as it arrives (feature 010), so spoken output from the map must be coordinated with that channel — repository history, commit `7a9c93a` in this repository

### Inferences
- The room graph is its own best accessible structure. Each room becomes an accessibility element (via `accessibilityChildren`) labelled, for example, "Kitchen. Current location. Exits: north to Hallway, east unexplored, down to Cellar." with custom actions "Go here" and one action per exit. This is "perceptually congruent" navigation in Benthic's sense: the user moves through the map along the same exits a sighted user sees.
- Order matters: VoiceOver swipe order should not be raw screen order. Sensible orders are: current room first, then neighbours by compass order, then the rest by distance in steps; plus custom rotors for "unexplored exits", "regions" and "rooms by name". A plain searchable list of rooms ("places I have been") is the fallback that always works and doubles as a jump-to control for everyone.
- An overview sentence on the map container meets the "overview first" recommendation: "Map, 23 rooms on 2 levels, 4 unexplored exits. You are in the Kitchen, ground level."
- Announcements when the map grows should be brief, and should not talk over the game's reply; the simplest rule is to append the map change to the same announcement ("New place: Cellar, below Kitchen") or to omit it when the game text already names the room.
- Audio graphs (pitch against an x axis) do not correspond to anything meaningful in a room graph; TADA's use of tones for node-link diagrams suggests optional sonification (for example, a tone when a finger passes over a room in touch exploration), but that is an enhancement, not the base.
- Dynamic Type: room labels drawn in Canvas must be scaled by reading the dynamic type size and resolving text at that size; at large sizes, labels will not fit in room boxes, so the semantic-zoom tiers should shift (fewer rooms on screen, labels only for the current room and neighbours, full names in the detail sheet). The list view carries full text at any size.
- Reduce Motion: replace the stretch animation, the "pencil drawing in" effect and animated camera follow with cross-fades or instant changes; keep a brief highlight on the new room so the change is still noticed. Honour Increase Contrast and Differentiate Without Colour too: a pencil-on-paper palette is low-contrast by nature, so a high-contrast ink variant is needed, and state (current, visited, unexplored) must be carried by shape or line style, not colour alone.

### Gaps
- Apple's documentation for `accessibilityChildren`, custom rotors (`accessibilityRotor`), custom actions and the `accessibilityReduceMotion` and `dynamicTypeSize` environment values was not fetched this session; the API names in the inferences are from background knowledge and should be checked against current documentation for the deployment target.
- Whether VoiceOver's direct-touch exploration works well over `accessibilityChildren` frames positioned to match rooms drawn in a Canvas, including while the canvas is panned and zoomed, was not confirmed; it needs a prototype.
- "Rich Screen Reader Experiences for Accessible Data Visualization" (Zong et al., 2022) and Olli were only seen through Benthic's description of them. Unverified from memory: Zong et al. propose three design dimensions (structure, navigation, description) for screen-reader visualisation.
- No research was found on blind players' use of maps in text adventures or MUDs specifically. Unverified: MUD clients aimed at blind players favour textual "where is" and speed-walk commands over spatial maps; this would be worth a targeted look.
- No sources were gathered on accessibility of indoor maps in Apple Maps or MapKit with VoiceOver.
