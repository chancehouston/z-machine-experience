# Text adventure geography and the culture of maps

Reader's note on reliability. Pages were read through a fetch tool that summarises, so wording inside quotation marks is as that tool relayed it and should be checked against the page before being printed as a verbatim quote. Each "Gaps" section separates what no source confirmed in this session from what was found. Several well-known game mechanics (the carousel room, the Royal Puzzle, shipboard directions in Starcross) could not be sourced in the time available and are listed in Gaps as unverified background, not as findings.

## 1. Game by game: what is unusual about each game's geography or movement?

### Takeaway
The sourced material shows at least eight distinct kinds of space a map must cope with: hand-mapped caves with mazes and one-way passages (Zork, Adventure), a house with scheduled characters and sightlines (Deadline), a complex where exits are never described and six pieces are tracked at once (Suspended), places reached only by an object or a word (Spellbreaker's cubes, Adventure's xyzzy, Jigsaw's hub), a space that swaps east and west (Trinity), the same map across eras (AMFV, Wishbringer), geography reshuffled on restart or restore (Beyond Zork), and disconnected scenes with no map to speak of (Photopia, Nord and Bert, Border Zone). Many of the specific set pieces named in the brief were not confirmed by a source in this session; see Gaps.

### Cited Findings

**Zork I, II, III**
- The white house in Zork I is surrounded by "plenty of difficult-to-map forest", a carry-over from Adventure, and the house acts as a "de facto staging area" for the underground. — [Digital Antiquarian, Exploring Zork Part 1](https://www.filfre.net/2012/01/exploring-zork-part-1/)
- Going down the trap door closes the way back behind the player (the door is barred), so the first descent is one-way until another exit is found. — [Digital Antiquarian, Exploring Zork Part 1](https://www.filfre.net/2012/01/exploring-zork-part-1/)
- Emily Short describes Zork's house and garden paths as a textbook case of asymmetric exits, notes its one-way ring passages (trap door, fireplace) that control direction of travel, and notes that the Cyclops smashing a wall creates a shortcut, that is, the map gains a connection during play. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)
- Zork I's maze is described by Jimmy Maher as the only large, fully traditional maze in the Infocom catalogue; the thief wanders it and scatters the items a player drops to mark rooms, which undermines the standard mapping method. — [Digital Antiquarian, Exploring Zork Part 3 (via search result)](https://www.filfre.net/tag/zork/page/3)
- A thief wanders the underground in Zork I, and dark areas need a light source. In Zork III an earthquake occurs after about 130 moves, "opening one passageway and closing another"; this timed change to the map was new to the series. — [Wikipedia, Zork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zork_III)
- Zork II has a puzzle whose difficulty and reliance on baseball knowledge Infocom's own hint book apologised for; the volcano, coal mine, bank and Royal Zork Puzzle Museum sections were added to the mainframe game in 1977 and 1978. — [Wikipedia, Zork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zork_II)
- The baseball maze was not in the PDP-10 Zork; Lebling devised it for the microcomputer version. — [search result summarising Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/tag/zork/page/3)
- Players can go through the world and solve puzzles in almost any order, though some passages need a puzzle solved first. — [Wikipedia, Zork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zork_II)

**Colossal Cave / Adventure**
- Ten rooms share the identical description "YOU ARE IN A MAZE OF TWISTY LITTLE PASSAGES, ALL ALIKE"; the layout is fixed, so the player must work out a way to map it. — [Wikipedia, Colossal Cave Adventure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossal_Cave_Adventure)
- "xyzzy" teleports the player between the building and the debris room; Crowther added it at his sister's request so she could skip the early part of the game. — [Wikipedia, Colossal Cave Adventure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossal_Cave_Adventure)
- Crowther's cave follows the real Bedquilt section of Mammoth Cave closely, with some passages removed; Woods's additions do not correspond to the real cave. — [Wikipedia, Colossal Cave Adventure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossal_Cave_Adventure)
- Graham Nelson calls Adventure a very "wide" game (much of the map open at once) and Spellbreaker a very "narrow" one. — [Graham Nelson, The Craft of Adventure](https://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/info/Craft.Of.Adventure.txt)

**Enchanter**
- Enchanter's Hall of Mirrors is a single place implemented as several locations, and its long road leading nowhere is a device for limiting the frontier that Short treats as a negative example. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)

**Deadline, The Witness, Suspect**
- Deadline's characters "moved around the mansion on their own schedule". The game holds internal routing tables that let a character walk to a destination (the source calls it the "T system"), a daily schedule for the whole cast, and sightline data that "allow you to see a character in adjacent rooms down the hall". Andrew Plotkin's Visible Deadline (June 2026) shows these as blue routing paths and pink sightlines on a Map tab, with a separate Schedule tab. — [Andrew Plotkin, Visible Deadline](https://blog.zarfhome.com/2026/06/visible-deadline)
- Deadline depends on timed events that need the player in an exact place at an exact time; The Witness is described as having no such events. — [search result, retrospective review](https://www.justgamesretro.com/?p=789)
- Deadline has about 50 locations split between the inside of the house and its grounds. — [search result, review](https://everygamegoing.com/larticle/Deadline-000/27811)
- Short cites Deadline as balancing hallway space against the rooms that hang off it. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)

**Suspended**
- The player never moves. The character lies in suspended animation and acts only by ordering robots about an underground complex. — [Wikipedia, Suspended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspended_(video_game))
- Room descriptions "do not mention the directions of possible exits", which makes the printed map necessary to play. — [Wikipedia, Suspended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspended_(video_game))
- Each robot perceives the same room through a different sense: Iris sees (and is confined to the area around the Central Chamber), Auda hears, Sensa detects magnetic and photon emissions, Poet senses electrical flow and speaks in metaphor, Waldo manipulates and perceives by sonar, Whiz talks to the library computer. — [Wikipedia, Suspended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspended_(video_game)); [Digital Antiquarian, Suspended](https://www.filfre.net/?p=879)
- The box held "a map of the complex in the form of a game board, with a set of counters representing each of the robots", and "the board quickly becomes an essential tool for keeping track of the whole situation". Orders can go to several robots at once. — [Digital Antiquarian, Suspended](https://www.filfre.net/?p=879)
- Maher calls it "more a strategy game implemented in text than an interactive fiction". — [Digital Antiquarian, Suspended](https://www.filfre.net/?p=879)
- In the digital collections the board is only a scanned image with no interactivity, and one player calls the counters page "pretty useless" in that form. — [intfiction.org, Question about classic IF games](https://intfiction.org/t/question-about-classic-if-games/7946)

**Trinity**
- The opening is a closed vignette in Kensington Gardens; the body of the game is a magical landscape that acts as a hub from which the historical scenes branch. — [Digital Antiquarian, Trinity](https://www.filfre.net/2015/01/trinity/)
- Plotkin says "The best-known Klein bottle is in Trinity", that it covers "just a few rooms" and not the whole game, and that the game shows the reversal with "variable room descriptions that change 'east' to 'west'". — [intfiction.org, Directions on the Surface of a Klein Bottle](https://intfiction.org/t/directions-on-the-surface-of-a-klein-bottle/66694)
- Another poster in that discussion argues the Trinity space is closer to a Möbius strip than a Klein bottle. — [intfiction.org, same thread, page 2](https://intfiction.org/t/directions-on-the-surface-of-a-klein-bottle/66694?page=2)
- Nelson's remark, quoted by Short, faults Trinity's middle game for feeling like a square grid. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)

**A Mind Forever Voyaging**
- The player walks the city of Rockvil in simulation mode by typing directions; the simulations are projections for 2041, 2051, 2061, 2071 and 2081. The box held a map of Rockvil. — [Wikipedia, A Mind Forever Voyaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Mind_Forever_Voyaging)
- It has many rooms (178) and few objects (30). — [Z-Machine Standards, Appendix E](https://inform-fiction.org/zmachine/standards/z1point0/appe.html)

**Wishbringer**
- The village of Festeron is transformed during play into a sinister counterpart, Witchville. The box held a postal zone map of Festeron. — [Wikipedia, Wishbringer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wishbringer)
- Short praises it as playable for novices: a central rotary as a hub, the coastline as a boundary, and a paper street map in the box. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)

**Spellbreaker**
- The game centres on sixteen small white cubes; casting a spell on one transports the player to a room with several exits. — [search result, IFWiki Spellbreaker and walkthroughs](https://ifwiki.org/Spellbreaker)
- One puzzle requires the player to make a map of a set of lines in order to trap a moving rock. — [search result, walkthrough](https://www.walkthroughking.com/text/spellbreaker.aspx)
- Nelson's verdict that Spellbreaker is very "narrow", and Short's that its teleporting cubes make each solved puzzle rewarding because it opens new map. — [Craft of Adventure](https://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/info/Craft.Of.Adventure.txt); [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)

**Beyond Zork**
- The game draws a map on screen as the player explores ("auto-mapping"). After a character dies and the player restarts, "the locations of rooms and encounters are all randomly redistributed". Reviewers at the time complained that maps and item properties re-randomised on restoring a save, and warned that "rooms previously mapped have now changed". Z-code version 5. The box held a fold-out map of the Southland of Quendor. — [Wikipedia, Beyond Zork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beyond_Zork)

**Starcross, Seastalker**
- Starcross shipped with a partial space map of charted masses, used with the in-game navigation computer. — [Wikipedia, Starcross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starcross_(video_game))
- Seastalker's map came in the game box, not in the hint kit. — [search result quoting an InvisiClues order form](https://www.mocagh.org/infocom/invisiclues.pdf)

**Nord and Bert, Border Zone, Hitchhiker's**
- Nord and Bert is eight independent short stories; the first seven can be played in any order and the eighth unlocks with passwords from the others. — [Wikipedia, Nord and Bert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nord_and_Bert_Couldn%27t_Make_Head_or_Tail_of_It)
- Border Zone has three chapters, each with a different player character, and runs in real time (the clock runs while the game waits for input). The box held a map of the border. — [Wikipedia, Border Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Zone_(video_game))
- The Hitchhiker's Guide has only 31 internal rooms against 45 takeable objects. — [Infocom Fact Sheet](https://mirror.ifarchive.org/if-archive/infocom/info/fact-sheet.txt)

**Later Inform-era games**
- Jigsaw is a hub map: the time zones are reached only from the central time-machine room, and sections open gradually. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)
- Photopia is made of disjunct segments tied to story scenes, "like cuts in a film"; such maps make mapping unnecessary. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)
- Curses allows flexible puzzle order and gives the player teleportation powers as shortcuts. It has a "places" command listing every location visited. It begins in the attic of a house. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/); [Wikipedia, Curses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curses_(video_game))
- Anchorhead uses coastlines to discourage exploring in some directions and has moving characters; one player reports keeping its whole geography "laid out in my head" without a map. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/); [intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/question-about-classic-if-games/7946)
- Counterfeit Monkey is set on an island city and was written in Inform 7. Its source lists a separate local map figure for each location, and its design notes say the aim of the graphics is to give the player visual orientation, borrowing from commercial minimaps that show goal locations. — [Wikipedia, Counterfeit Monkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterfeit_Monkey); [Counterfeit Monkey source listing](https://tass.neocities.org/source/source_3.html)
- Other patterns Short names from the Inform era: the same map across several eras with changes rippling forward (First Things First), descriptions that change over time (A Change in the Weather), the same places seen through different characters (Being Andrew Plotkin), a map on a tiny planet (Small World), rooms arranged round a sphere (The Meteor, the Stone and a Long Glass of Sherbet), and an ASCII map supplied by the game (Christminster). — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)

### Inferences
- Suspended breaks the assumption that a map has one "you are here". It needs six markers, and the player's own position is not on the map at all.
- Deadline shows that a map of a house may need a time axis and other people's positions, and that the story file itself contains routing and sightline tables that an interpreter-side tool can read (Visible Deadline does so from source; whether it can be done from the compiled file alone was not established).
- Trinity shows that the direction printed in the text can differ from the direction on a fixed diagram, so a map built from "player typed EAST and arrived in room X" will draw a mirrored region unless it allows a room to appear in two orientations.
- Beyond Zork shows that a remembered map can be invalidated by restart or restore, so any stored map must be tied to a particular playthrough, not to the game file alone.
- Beyond Zork is evidence that Infocom itself built a self-revealing map in a Z-machine version 5 game in 1987.

### Gaps
No source read in this session confirmed the following, though all are widely described and worth checking against the game files in the corpus, InvisiClues scans or walkthroughs before they are relied on:
- Zork I: the boat and Frigid River as a one-way vehicle route. Zork II: the carousel room that scrambles exits until stopped, the balloon in the volcano, the Wizard moving the player. Zork III: the Royal Puzzle as a sliding-block grid, the time machine, the Scenic Vista table.
- Adventure: the second maze ("all different"), and PLUGH and PLOVER as further teleport words. The Wikipedia page read mentioned only the "all alike" maze and xyzzy.
- Enchanter and Sorcerer: the translucent rooms, the glass maze and the time-travel puzzle. Nothing found.
- Planetfall and Stationfall: nothing found beyond room counts. (Background, unverified: two complexes joined by a shuttle in Planetfall; a station that becomes more dangerous over days in Stationfall.)
- The Witness and Suspect: nothing found on their floor plans or schedules beyond The Witness lacking timed events.
- Starcross and Seastalker: no source confirmed the shipboard direction words (fore, aft, port, starboard) or Starcross's cylinder geometry. The Wikipedia page is silent.
- The Hitchhiker's Guide as a set of scenes, and Nord and Bert's lack of compass movement: only the room count and the chapter structure were confirmed.
- The Lurking Horror, Spider and Web, Lost Pig, Bronze: nothing found on their geography. Hadean Lands: the Wikipedia page returned an error and the search found nothing, so its map and travel commands are unconfirmed. (Background, unverified: Hadean Lands and Counterfeit Monkey are Glulx games, not Z-machine, so they fall outside the version 1 to 8 scope although they are relevant as design examples.)
- The exact rule by which Trinity swaps directions, and which rooms it affects. Plotkin points to a "Let's Play: Trinity" thread on intfiction.org that was not read.

## 2. How large are these games in rooms?

### Takeaway
A reliable per-game table exists: the Infocom Fact Sheet counts internal rooms from the story files. The catalogue runs from 30 rooms (The Witness, Seastalker) to 215 (Zork Zero), with version 3 games averaging 69 and version 4 and 5 games 105. Internal counts are not the same as what a player perceives.

### Cited Findings
- Internal room counts and takeable-object counts by game (release in brackets): Zork I 110 / 60; Zork II 86 / 50; Zork III 89 / 23; Deadline 51 / 37; Starcross 86 / 25; Suspended 63 / 33; The Witness 30 / 22; Planetfall 105 / 45; Enchanter 74 / 33; Infidel 77 / 57; Sorcerer 84 / 36; Seastalker 30 / 15; Cutthroats 68 / 21; Hitchhiker's 31 / 45; Suspect 57 / 43; Wishbringer 52 / 35; A Mind Forever Voyaging 178 / 30; Spellbreaker 79 / 60; Ballyhoo 36 / 42; Trinity 134 / 49; Leather Goddesses 75 / 41; Moonmist 69 / 26; Hollywood Hijinx 67 / 58; Bureaucracy 50 / 44; Stationfall 105 / 53; Lurking Horror 71 / 44; Nord and Bert 41 / 69; Plundered Hearts 57 / 28; Beyond Zork 128 / 77; Border Zone 111 / 42; Mini-Zork I 69 / 46; Sherlock 92 / 67; Zork Zero 215 / 106; Shogun 75 / 63; Arthur 90 / 32. — [Infocom Fact Sheet 4.95, Paul David Doherty, 22 June 2007](https://mirror.ifarchive.org/if-archive/infocom/info/fact-sheet.txt)
- The fact sheet warns that the count is the internal representation and a player may perceive fewer or more rooms; Infidel's endless desert is made of only 10 internal rooms. — [Infocom Fact Sheet](https://mirror.ifarchive.org/if-archive/infocom/info/fact-sheet.txt)
- The average for version 3 games is 69 rooms and 39 takeable objects; for version 4 and 5 games, 105 rooms and 54 objects. — [Z-Machine Standards, Appendix E](https://inform-fiction.org/zmachine/standards/z1point0/appe.html)
- A fan mapping project put Zork Zero at 208 rooms against the fact sheet's 215, so counts vary with method. — [search result, superverbose.com](https://inventory.superverbose.com/tag/activision/)
- A forum table gives The Lurking Horror 75 rooms, against the fact sheet's 71. — [AtariAge forum](https://forums.atariage.com/topic/310740-infocom-small-systems-development/); contradicted by [Infocom Fact Sheet](https://mirror.ifarchive.org/if-archive/infocom/info/fact-sheet.txt)
- Crowther's original Adventure has descriptions for 66 rooms; Woods's 1977 version has 140 map locations. — [Wikipedia, Colossal Cave Adventure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossal_Cave_Adventure)
- Nelson says older games often had about 200 rooms with thin content, and advises a budget of about 60 rooms at roughly four objects to a room, with a prologue of at most eight to ten rooms and about 50 locations in the middle game. — [Craft of Adventure](https://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/info/Craft.Of.Adventure.txt)
- Nelson: a location usually stands for a room about ten yards across at most, and large chambers are usually built from several locations. — [Craft of Adventure](https://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/info/Craft.Of.Adventure.txt)

### Inferences
- A general map should expect anything from about 30 to about 220 nodes, with 50 to 110 typical. The two smallest counts belong to games that came with a printed map (Seastalker) or are set in one house (The Witness).
- Room count is a poor guide to how much map a player needs. Border Zone has 111 internal rooms across three short chapters, and Hitchhiker's has 31 rooms yet is a long game. A high count with few objects (AMFV, 178 / 30) marks a place meant for walking and observing.
- Internal rooms can stand for one perceived place (Infidel's desert, Enchanter's Hall of Mirrors), so a map that draws one box per internal room object can mislead in both directions.

### Gaps
- No room counts were found for Curses, Anchorhead, Jigsaw, Photopia, Spider and Web, Counterfeit Monkey, Hadean Lands, Lost Pig or Bronze. IFDB and the IF Archive's inform-gamelist were not consulted.
- The fact sheet's method (how it decides an object is a room) was not examined.

## 3. General patterns

### Takeaway
Emily Short's essay gives a ready taxonomy of layouts (hub, formal symmetry, street map, disjunct segments, hybrids) and of gates, and Graham Nelson's gives the structural view (prologue, middle game and end game sealed from each other; wide against narrow). Between them and the game notes above, every pattern in the brief has at least one sourced example except shipboard directions.

### Cited Findings
- Layout types: hub (one location links otherwise unconnected sections), formal symmetry (including floors that share a plan), street map (arterials and cul-de-sacs), disjunct segments (small maps joined by plot or vehicle with no geographic connection), and hybrids, which most large games are. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)
- Regions: named districts and continuous features such as rivers, coastlines and cliffs give a level of landscape between the room and the whole map. Distant landmarks orient the player. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)
- Gates: block-points (a barrier needing an item from elsewhere), semi-blocks (solvable on the spot), continuous need (equipment such as light required throughout an area), and limited access (timed entry or restricted inventory). Short's pacing rule is to open no more than six or seven new rooms at a time. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)
- Edges: maps hide their edges with natural borders, long roads, rows of uninteresting doors, and described vistas of places that cannot be entered (desert, forest, chasm). So Far has a locked door with voices behind it, suggesting space without offering it. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)
- Phases: a game has "a prologue, a middle game and an end game, usually quite closed off from each other", and a phase once left generally cannot be returned to. It is common to have a room that can be visited only once. — [Craft of Adventure](https://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/info/Craft.Of.Adventure.txt)
- Shape: Nelson wants a continuous map, some connections in the diagonal directions so it does not read as a square grid, a few long loops, no "bus service map" of lines meeting at one exchange, and a rapid way across a very large map. — [Craft of Adventure](https://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/info/Craft.Of.Adventure.txt)
- Mazes: Nelson says "Almost every game contains a maze", that the standard solution is to drop objects to tell rooms apart, and that a maze should have a new twist or be left out. Short says mazes defeat the player's ability to navigate and that recent advice is to avoid them. — [Craft of Adventure](https://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/info/Craft.Of.Adventure.txt); [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)
- Shortcuts and travel commands: Short recommends letting the player go to any visited location with one command (her example is GO TO THEATER); Curses uses teleportation as a shortcut; ADRIFT has automatic mapping with click-to-travel. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)
- Maps and puzzles: in classic parser games the companion of the map was the puzzle dependency chart; the chart showed which barriers came before which, and the map showed how that lay in space. — [Emily Short, Mailbag: Teaching Spatial Storytelling](https://emshort.blog/2017/04/21/mailbag-teaching-spatial-storytelling/)
- Players use the map as a to-do list: Nelson says the main technique is "to look at their maps and see what's left that they don't understand". — [Craft of Adventure](https://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/info/Craft.Of.Adventure.txt)

Pattern index, with the sourced example for each (sources as given in section 1):
- Regions and phases sealed from each other: Trinity (vignette, hub, scenes), Nord and Bert (eight stories), Border Zone (three chapters).
- Vehicles and disjunct segments: Short's "disjunct segments" class; Photopia.
- Places reached only by magic or an object: Adventure (xyzzy), Spellbreaker (cubes), Jigsaw (time-machine hub), Curses (teleport powers).
- Places that change over time: Zork III (earthquake), Zork I (Cyclops wall), Wishbringer (town transformed), AMFV (five decades), Beyond Zork (reshuffled on restart).
- Places seen but not reachable: Short's vistas and locked doors; Deadline's sightlines down a hall.
- One-way and asymmetric exits: Zork I (trap door, fireplace, garden paths).
- Exits not stated in the text: Suspended.
- Direction words that lie: Trinity.
- Several pieces on the board, or other people on it: Suspended, Deadline.
- One place built of several rooms, or several perceived places built of few: Enchanter's Hall of Mirrors; Infidel's desert.
- Almost no geography: Photopia; Hitchhiker's (31 rooms).

### Inferences
- A map model that copes with the catalogue needs, at minimum: directed edges (not assumed reversible); edges labelled by any command, not only compass words; nodes that are not joined to anything by ordinary movement; separate sheets or regions with no implied spatial relation; a way to record that a node or edge changed or disappeared; and more than one marker.
- The six-or-seven-rooms rule and Nelson's sealed phases suggest that a map revealing itself as the player explores will usually grow in small bursts, and will sometimes have to start a fresh sheet when a phase ends.
- Because players read a map for "what's left", unexplored exits are as much a part of the map as rooms. Hand-drawn maps show these as stubs.
- Mazes of identical rooms cannot be told apart by name alone; a map keyed on room name will merge them, and one keyed on the internal object will solve the maze for the player. That is a design decision, not a technical one.

### Gaps
- Non-compass movement (in, out, enter, climb, shipboard words) was not covered by any source read. The IFWiki "Geography" page is only a short glossary entry.
- Vertical levels were touched only through Short's point about floors sharing a plan. No source discussed how up and down were drawn.
- Nick Montfort's Twisty Little Passages and Andrew Plotkin's own essays on geography were not consulted. Paul O'Brian's "Landscape and Character in IF" was found by title only.

## 4. How maps were part of the original experience

### Takeaway
Infocom treated the map as the player's job and sold the answer separately. Line-and-box maps were hint material, first from Infocom in 1981, then from the Zork Users Group, then folded into InvisiClues kits, each with a warning that the map gives too much away. Maps in the box were a different thing: in-world documents (a postal map, a city map, blueprints, a border map) or, in Suspended, a working game board.

### Cited Findings
- The Zork Users Group was spun out of Infocom by Mike Dornbrook in 1981 to sell hints, maps and other goods, and was absorbed back in 1983. It was the origin of InvisiClues and the New Zork Times. — [IFWiki, Zork Users Group](https://www.ifwiki.org/Zork_Users_Group)
- Lebling drew a fair-copy map of mainframe Zork in 1978 during development. — [Andrew Plotkin, A treasury of Zork maps](https://blog.zarfhome.com/2023/02/a-treasury-of-zork-maps)
- Infocom's first hint map (David Ardito and Dave Lebling, 1981) was advertised inside the earliest releases of the game as the "Movement Assistance Planner (MAP)", alongside "HINTS". It is the only Infocom map that predates the Zork Users Group. — [Andrew Plotkin, A treasury of Zork maps](https://blog.zarfhome.com/2023/02/a-treasury-of-zork-maps)
- The 1981 Zork I poster by Ardito is not a true map but a set of recognisable scenes with some spatial links visible. — [Andrew Plotkin, A treasury of Zork maps](https://blog.zarfhome.com/2023/02/a-treasury-of-zork-maps)
- The 1982 Zork Users Group map of Zork I (Ardito and Steve Meretzky) uses line-and-box diagrams in the manner of hand-drawn scrap-paper maps, decorated with small scenes. — [Andrew Plotkin, A treasury of Zork maps](https://blog.zarfhome.com/2023/02/a-treasury-of-zork-maps)
- Dornbrook had Meretzky draw a Zork II map to sell beside the Zork I map. The Zork II poster (Pier Giovanni Binotti, 1983) is a fine ink drawing, 22 by 28 inches, covering the whole geography of the game. — [Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/?p=625); [Crouch Rare Books listing](https://crouchrarebooks.com/browse/at-last-a-zork-ii-poster/?print=pdf)
- After 1983 Infocom's own Zork I hint map kept the diagrams but replaced the art with a stone-wall texture; it stayed in use through the Lost Treasures and Masterpieces collections and the 2012 iOS release. — [Andrew Plotkin, A treasury of Zork maps](https://blog.zarfhome.com/2023/02/a-treasury-of-zork-maps)
- An InvisiClues kit held a hint book, a latent-image marker and a map, except for Suspended and Seastalker, whose maps were in the game box. — [Infocom InvisiClues order form](https://www.mocagh.org/infocom/invisiclues.pdf)
- The booklets warned that "special caution should be exerted with regard to the maps. If used too early in the course of your travels, they'll definitely give away too much." — [Zork I InvisiClues scan (via search result)](https://mocagh.org/infocom/zork-hintbook.pdf)
- Plotkin: line-and-bubble maps are hint material and did not come with the games; looking at one counts as peeking; the games expected players to draw their own. Another poster: maps packed with the game, as in Moonmist or Wishbringer, can be essential. — [intfiction.org, Question about classic IF games](https://intfiction.org/t/question-about-classic-if-games/7946)
- Infocom sold fold-out "Blueprints" for Deadline and The Witness: illustrated 11 by 17 inch maps showing all locations and passageways. — [Infocom catalogue](https://mocagh.org/infocom/infocom-catalog3.pdf)
- Maps in the box: Wishbringer (postal zone map), AMFV (map of Rockvil), Border Zone (map of the border), Beyond Zork (fold-out map of the Southland of Quendor), Starcross (space chart), Suspended (board and counters). — [Wikipedia: Wishbringer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wishbringer), [AMFV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Mind_Forever_Voyaging), [Border Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Zone_(video_game)), [Beyond Zork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beyond_Zork), [Starcross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starcross_(video_game)); [Digital Antiquarian, Suspended](https://www.filfre.net/?p=879)
- Outside Infocom: Steven Roy's 1982 diagram of mainframe Dungeon, "thoughtfully designed with a map-like layout"; line-and-box maps in cheat files and in Kim Schuette's Book of Adventure Games (1984). — [Andrew Plotkin, A treasury of Zork maps](https://blog.zarfhome.com/2023/02/a-treasury-of-zork-maps)
- Later fan maps move away from the diagram: a 2014 cutaway illustration containing every Zork I location with the mazes condensed, and Keith Orlando's 2023 isometric painted map, in which treasures glow gold. — [Andrew Plotkin, A treasury of Zork maps](https://blog.zarfhome.com/2023/02/a-treasury-of-zork-maps)
- Collections of scans: Plotkin's index of Zork and Adventure maps, the IF Archive's maps directories, and the Museum of Computer Adventure Game History's hint book scans. — [eblong.com](https://eblong.com/infocom/maps/); [IF Archive](https://mirror.ifarchive.org/if-archive/solutions/maps/Index); [mocagh.org](https://mocagh.org/infocom/zork-hintbook.pdf)

### Inferences
- There were two families of period map. The schematic (boxes and lines) was a spoiler and was sold as one. The pictorial or in-world map (poster, postal map, blueprint) set the scene and gave little away. A self-revealing map sits between them: schematic in form, but it shows only what the player has earned.
- Infocom's own practice supports revealing nothing ahead of the player. The official maps came with a warning, and the community still treats looking at one as taking a hint.
- The condensing of mazes on the 2014 cutaway map, and the decorated scenes on the 1982 map, show that published maps did not give every room equal weight.
- Suspended and Beyond Zork are the two cases where Infocom judged that the player should not have to draw the map: one because the text withholds exits, the other because the geography moves.

### Gaps
- The drawing conventions asked about (dotted or arrowed lines for one-way passages, labels for objects in rooms, marks for up and down, how mazes were laid out) were not confirmed by any source. The scans at mocagh.org and eblong.com are images and were not viewed. This needs a direct look at the Zork I and II InvisiClues maps.
- Magazine-published maps: nothing found.
- No InvisiClues map other than Zork's was described by a source.
- Sizes and print details of the Zork Users Group Zork I map were not found.

## 5. What players remember and value about mapping by hand

### Takeaway
The first-hand accounts found are few but consistent: drawing the map was a large part of the pleasure, the map was a personal artefact that many kept (and others regret losing), and automatic mapping is felt as a convenience that cost something. A minority never mapped at all.

### Cited Findings
- Creole_Ned, who drew elaborate maps for Zork, Infidel, Cutthroats and Spellbreaker and kept nearly all of them in a sketchbook, with lists of saved games and spells and copied box lettering: "I think half the enjoyment I got from these games was doodling the maps." — [Quarter to Three forum](https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/hand-drawn-game-maps-or-why-does-my-hovel-look-like-an-igloo/66134)
- Aristophan, on the Zork maze: "knowing to drop items in rooms was the only way to truly map it." — [Quarter to Three forum](https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/hand-drawn-game-maps-or-why-does-my-hovel-look-like-an-igloo/66134)
- BigWeather, on automatic mapping being expected now: "it still feels like we've lost something". — [Quarter to Three forum](https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/hand-drawn-game-maps-or-why-does-my-hovel-look-like-an-igloo/66134)
- Calistas, on old notebook maps, proposing an exhibit of them: "I bet they are all destroyed now." Marcin: "Mine were all on grid paper and very simple, reflecting the medium." Bill_Dungsroman used graph paper for the Zork games. — [Quarter to Three forum](https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/hand-drawn-game-maps-or-why-does-my-hovel-look-like-an-igloo/66134)
- Andrew Plotkin, in a comment on Zork I: he "always figured the map was the twentieth treasure." — [Digital Antiquarian, Exploring Zork Part 1, comments](https://www.filfre.net/2012/01/exploring-zork-part-1/)
- A reader there plays in verbose mode because it helps her keep "better track of where I am on the map." — [Digital Antiquarian, Exploring Zork Part 1, comments](https://www.filfre.net/2012/01/exploring-zork-part-1/)
- tgl, on playing with ready-made maps: it "does not feel like the original experience", but drawing on a phone or tablet is impractical, so they use them reluctantly and plan to use pencil and paper for games they do not know. — [intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/question-about-classic-if-games/7946)
- namekuseijin never uses maps, and keeps Anchorhead's geography in their head. — [intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/question-about-classic-if-games/7946)
- A Metafilter commenter recalls a father and fellow engineers mapping Colossal Cave as they played, with maps that came out nearly identical. — [Metafilter, Zork Map (via search result; page itself refused the fetch)](https://www.metafilter.com/64803/Zork-Map)
- A hand-drawn Zork I "Dungeon Map" dated 1979 to 1982 survives and has been published online. — [2 Warps to Neptune](https://2warpstoneptune.com/2015/05/13/hand-drawn-dungeon-map-1979-1981)
- Short observes that modern players are less patient with mapping than players of the older classics, and prefers layouts that need no paper map. — [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)

### Inferences
- Three things are valued: the act of drawing (Creole_Ned), the map as proof of progress and a thing owned (Plotkin's "twentieth treasure", the kept sketchbooks), and the map as a working tool for noticing what is unexplained (Nelson, in section 3).
- tgl's account names the practical reason hand mapping has faded on phones and tablets, which is the platform this project targets: there is nowhere convenient to draw. A map that builds itself from the player's own moves answers that without handing over a published map.
- Players personalised their maps with notes, spell lists and lettering. A map that allows the player's own marks would keep some of what the accounts say was lost.
- Mapping is not universal. Some players keep the geography in their heads, so a map should be something a player may ignore.

### Gaps
- Get Lamp interview material on mapping was not found; the search returned only general pages about the film.
- Only a handful of accounts were found, from two forum threads and one comment section. They lean towards people who enjoyed mapping enough to join a thread about it.
- The Metafilter thread could not be read directly (HTTP 403), so its one account rests on a search summary.
- No account was found from anyone who disliked mapping at the time, apart from Short's general remark and the reviewers' complaints about Beyond Zork.
