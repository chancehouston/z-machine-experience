# Reading memory fixes identity, not layout

A self-revealing map as the main screen of a parser-game app has no direct precedent, but nearly every part of it has one, and the record says where the risk is. Every general-purpose automapper for text adventures and MUDs failed first on one question, "is this the same room?", and the app's engine answers that question exactly by reading the room object from memory each turn. What no tool has ever solved is the second question, "where do I draw it?": automatic layout of an irregular, direction-labelled graph is unsolved in every tool the notes examined, and the working answers are local views, separate sheets, bent or labelled connectors and a way for the player to correct the result. The four commercial text adventures that shipped a self-drawing map (1987 to 1993) converged on the same reveal rules, and critics called the map the one handy part of otherwise cluttered interfaces. The best video-game maps add the design stance: decide what feeling the map protects, keep the unknown visibly incomplete instead of blank, and let the machine draw geometry while the player owns the meaning. The games themselves demand more than a grid of boxes: directed exits, moves by any verb, places that change, more than one marker, and mazes where a perfect map is itself a spoiler. The evidence is strongest on prior art and on the geography of the Infocom catalogue, and thinnest on phone interaction, on rendering performance and on screen-reader use of a spatial map, where most of what follows is the researchers' inference and needs a prototype.

## Four commercial maps agreed on five rules and never led the screen

Four commercial lineages put a self-revealing map on screen. **Beyond Zork (1987)** kept a character-graphics map permanently in the upper right of a split screen: rooms as boxes, connections as lines, the current room highlighted, a question mark for dark rooms, a new line when a door opened, and exits shown for the current room and adjoining visited rooms only. With a mouse the player could click the map to move ([Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/2015/11/beyond-zork/)). **Zork Zero (1988)** put the map behind a MAP command on its own screen, showing visited locations in the general vicinity only, per its manual as seen in a search excerpt ([Zork Zero manual](https://www.mocagh.org/infocom/zorkzero-manual.pdf)). **Legend's games (from 1990)** swapped the map in for the illustration, and **Wonderland (1990)** made it one resizable window among many, with one-click travel to any location ([Digital Antiquarian on Legend](https://www.filfre.net/2017/01/a-time-of-beginnings-legend-entertainment-or-bob-and-mikes-excellent-adventure-game-company/); [Digital Antiquarian on Magnetic Scrolls](https://www.filfre.net/2017/01/a-time-of-endings-part-4-magnetic-scrolls/)).

The researchers' reading of these four is that they converge on five rules: reveal only what has been visited, show exits as stubs before the far room is known, mark the current room strongly, mark dark rooms as unknown and not absent, and let a tap on a known room travel there. That convergence is an inference from the descriptions, but it is a consistent one. The criticism is equally consistent and is not about the map. Jimmy Maher calls Beyond Zork's interface "hugely convenient" and wishes later games had built on it; his complaints about Legend and Wonderland are clutter and speed, and he calls the Legend automap "handy" though "not always quite enough to replace pen and paper" ([Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/2015/11/beyond-zork/); [Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/2017/01/a-time-of-beginnings-legend-entertainment-or-bob-and-mikes-excellent-adventure-game-company/)).

Two cautions come out of this history. First, **nobody made the map the main thing on screen**: it was a quarter of the screen, a mode, or one window. A map-first phone layout is new ground in this genre. Second, Beyond Zork shows what happens when prose leans on the map: switching to text-only mode made play "a bit more confusing than usual" because room descriptions no longer listed exits ([Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/2015/11/beyond-zork/)). The app plays unmodified story files, so the prose is intact, but the reverse holds: the text of some games never states exits at all. Both Infocom maps also showed only the local vicinity, which sidesteps the hardest layout problem and suits a small screen. Later examples exist but are thinly sourced here: Hadean Lands on iOS lists a built-in map with tap-to-travel to any explored room, and Counterfeit Monkey shades a clickable compass rose by whether each neighbour has been visited, both known from excerpts only ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/hadean-lands/id918958300); [Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2012/12/31/counterfeit-monkey/)).

## Every general automapper broke on room identity, which this engine reads directly

Outside per-game maps, there are four sources of map data, in rising order of reliability: scraping the transcript, reading the story's memory, having the game emit structured data, and having the author ship the map. The first is where most effort went and where it failed. Trizbort's own author says "automapping isn't a precise science" and that such tools "have trouble with mazes" ([intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/is-there-an-automapper-for-playing-z5-z-code-games/1894)). The best-documented scraper, scr2ifm, calls deciding whether a room was seen before its hardest step, and its manual lists the consequences: identical rooms merge into one, one-way links go undetected, teleport words create false links, walking into the dark creates a room called "Darkness", and all of it is repaired by hand in a separate command file ([IFM manual](https://ifm.readthedocs.io/en/latest/scr2ifm.html)).

MUD clients repeat the lesson at larger scale. zMUD matched rooms on text and "occasionally" stacked a second room on an existing one; it also had to keep a queue of unconfirmed moves, because a failed move ("The door is closed") otherwise created a room that did not exist ([zMUD help](https://zuggsoft.com/zmud/help6/Crea0191.htm); [zMUD help](https://zuggsoft.com/zmud/help6/Adva0177.htm)). Mapping became dependable only where the server sends a room ID out of band; one Mudlet project reported switching to server room IDs "after weeks of struggling with text-based detection" (forum excerpt) ([Mudlet forums](https://forums.mudlet.org/viewtopic.php?p=46753)). Andrew Plotkin summed up the single-player side: "Interpreters should do mapping, and also it's impossible for interpreters to do mapping" ([intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/coders-how-would-you-implement-a-tool-to-automap-as-you-play-i-f/48090?page=2)).

**The one prior interpreter that read memory, nitfol, is the closest ancestor of this app, and its failure list is the most useful document in the notes.** Nitfol needed the user to supply the location expression by hand, which its manual admits is "a lot of setup work", and it discovered exits by silently trying every direction and undoing. Its manual lists what still went wrong: random destinations, several places coded as one room (Enchanter's long road), exits that change state without moving the player, teleports, a fatal move recorded as leading to the reincarnation room, and games where the choice of location variable changes behaviour in the dark ([nitfol manual](https://0v9xf534pt.unbox.ifarchive.org/0v9xf534pt/nitfol.html)).

Because the app's engine already reads room identity, exits, doors, darkness and the object tree each turn, the largest error class disappears: identical maze rooms are distinct objects, renamed rooms keep their identity, and a failed move is simply "location unchanged". The zMUD lesson becomes a one-line rule: **commit a move to the map only after the location has actually changed.** What survives memory access, on the evidence above, is a shorter list. One internal object can stand for many perceived places, and several objects for one: Infidel's endless desert is 10 internal rooms, and Enchanter's Hall of Mirrors is one place built from several ([Infocom Fact Sheet](https://mirror.ifarchive.org/if-archive/infocom/info/fact-sheet.txt); [Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)). Darkness should appear as "somewhere dark", Beyond Zork's question mark, and resolve when light arrives. Knowing identity gives no position on the page. And every tool that inferred a map ended up needing a manual correction layer; the researchers' judgement, which the record supports, is that a design with no way to fix the map is betting on perfect inference that nobody has achieved.

The MUD world also supplies the parts that are treated as solved once identity is given. Mudlet stores rooms with stable integer IDs and integer coordinates, draws stub exits before the far room exists, splits the world into separate "areas", keeps "special exits" as command strings with no spatial direction, has a one-way symbol, and runs weighted A* pathfinding with lockable rooms and exits behind click-to-walk ([Mudlet manual](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper)). The non-spatial exit is the cleanest existing treatment of magic words, "enter" and "jump". The split between solved and unsolved is the researcher's inference from manuals and forum complaints, much of it 10 to 20 years old; no single community statement of it was found.

## The catalogue needs a directed, labelled, changeable graph of 30 to 215 rooms

Size is the best-sourced fact in the notes. The Infocom Fact Sheet counts internal rooms from the story files: **30 rooms (The Witness, Seastalker) to 215 (Zork Zero)**, with Zork I at 110, Trinity at 134 and A Mind Forever Voyaging at 178 ([Infocom Fact Sheet](https://mirror.ifarchive.org/if-archive/infocom/info/fact-sheet.txt)). Version 3 games average 69 rooms and version 4 and 5 games 105 ([Z-Machine Standards, Appendix E](https://inform-fiction.org/zmachine/standards/z1point0/appe.html)). Graham Nelson's advice to later authors is about 60 rooms ([The Craft of Adventure](https://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/info/Craft.Of.Adventure.txt)). No counts were found for the Inform-era games (Curses, Anchorhead, Jigsaw and others), so the upper bound for later work is unknown. Room count also predicts map need poorly: Hitchhiker's has 31 rooms and is a long game.

What strains a map is the variety of space. The table gathers the sourced cases; each one breaks an assumption a naive grid makes.

| What the game does | Sourced example | What the map must allow |
|---|---|---|
| One-way and asymmetric exits | Zork I's trap door closes behind the player; garden paths ([Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/2012/01/exploring-zork-part-1/); [Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)) | Directed edges, not assumed reversible |
| Travel by word or object | Adventure's xyzzy; Spellbreaker's cubes; Jigsaw's hub ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossal_Cave_Adventure); [Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)) | Edges labelled by any command, not drawn as a direction |
| The map changes during play | Zork III's earthquake opens one passage and closes another; the Cyclops wall; Wishbringer's transformed town ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zork_III); [Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)) | Edges and rooms that appear, vanish or change state |
| Geography reshuffled | Beyond Zork redistributes rooms on restart ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beyond_Zork)) | A stored map tied to one playthrough, not the game file |
| Direction words that lie | Trinity swaps east and west across a few rooms ([intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/directions-on-the-surface-of-a-klein-bottle/66694)) | A region that cannot be drawn consistently |
| Exits never described | Suspended's room text omits exits ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspended_(video_game))) | Exits taken from game data, not prose |
| Several pieces, or other people | Suspended's six robots; Deadline's scheduled cast ([Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/?p=879); [Visible Deadline](https://blog.zarfhome.com/2026/06/visible-deadline)) | More than one marker; the player may not be on the map |
| Sealed phases and disjoint scenes | Nord and Bert's eight stories; Border Zone's chapters; Photopia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nord_and_Bert_Couldn%27t_Make_Head_or_Tail_of_It); [Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)) | Separate sheets with no implied spatial relation |

Suspended and Deadline matter most for the "layers" ambition. Suspended shipped a board with counters because the player never moves and must track six robots, and Maher says the board "quickly becomes an essential tool"; an app that reads the object tree can place those counters itself. Deadline's story file holds routing tables, a daily schedule and sightline data, which Plotkin's Visible Deadline (June 2026) draws as paths and sightlines on a map; whether that data can be recovered from the compiled file alone, without source, was not established ([Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/?p=879); [Visible Deadline](https://blog.zarfhome.com/2026/06/visible-deadline)). Many famous set pieces the brief named were **not confirmed by any source**: Zork II's carousel room, the Royal Puzzle, the Frigid River boat, shipboard directions in Starcross and Seastalker, and Adventure's second maze. They should be checked against the corpus story files before the design relies on them.

Structure gives the map its rhythm. Emily Short classifies layouts as hub, formal symmetry, street map, disjunct segments and hybrids, and advises opening no more than six or seven rooms at a time; Nelson describes a prologue, middle game and end game "usually quite closed off from each other" ([Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/); [The Craft of Adventure](https://www.ifarchive.org/if-archive/info/Craft.Of.Adventure.txt)). A self-revealing map will therefore grow in small bursts and sometimes need a fresh sheet. Nelson also names the map's real job: players "look at their maps and see what's left that they don't understand". Unexplored exits are as much the content of the map as rooms.

The culture around maps sets the spoiler rule. Infocom sold line-and-box maps separately as hint material, with a printed warning that used too early "they'll definitely give away too much", and Plotkin says looking at one still counts as peeking ([Zork I InvisiClues](https://mocagh.org/infocom/zork-hintbook.pdf); [intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/question-about-classic-if-games/7946)). Forum opinion on automaps agrees on one thing: show only what the player has found ([intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/coders-how-would-you-implement-a-tool-to-automap-as-you-play-i-f/48090?page=2)). The objections are that drawing is "part of the adventure experience" and that maze connections are the puzzle; one player says half the enjoyment "was doodling the maps", while another notes that drawing on a phone is impractical ([intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/coders-how-would-you-implement-a-tool-to-automap-as-you-play-i-f/48090); [Quarter to Three](https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/hand-drawn-game-maps-or-why-does-my-hovel-look-like-an-igloo/66134); [intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/question-about-classic-if-games/7946)). These are a handful of accounts from self-selected threads; there is no survey data. The sharpest consequence is the maze. A map keyed on room objects draws every twisty passage distinctly and **solves the maze for the player**. In Zork I the thief scatters the objects a player drops as markers, so the maze is a designed difficulty. Whether to draw maze links, hide them or let the player choose is a design decision the sources assert but nobody has measured.

## The best game maps stay incomplete in ways that invite

Designers on record justify their maps by a feeling, not by convenience. Team Cherry built Hollow Knight "around this sense of discovery"; Etrian Odyssey's director wanted players to "relax, drink some coffee" and draw; Miasmata's designer says "getting lost is everything in our game" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/how-the-i-hollow-knight-i-devs-mapped-out-their-metroidvania-); [Director's Diary](https://www.dampfkraft.com/games/etrian-odyssey-directors-diary.html); [Low Cutoff](https://lowcutoff.blogspot.com/2013/06/bonus-feature-interview-with-miasmata.html)). The transferable measure comes from working cartographers at Stamen, who score game maps on being "incomplete": Breath of the Wild earns 10 out of 10 with a patterned "undiscovered" texture in place of fog, and Elden Ring's unrevealed map is "not a complete blank slate", with faint roads showing through ([Stamen on Breath of the Wild](https://stamen.com/cartographers-play-video-games-a-review-of-the-map-in-the-legend-of-zelda-breath-of-the-wild/); [Stamen on Elden Ring](https://stamen.com/cartographers-play-video-games-a-review-of-the-map-in-elden-ring/)). The unknown should hint at shape. For a text adventure the hint is the exit stub.

Failure cases define the other boundary. Robert Yang estimates he missed about a quarter of Subnautica's world because the game withdrew guidance without saying so, and Miasmata players could not tell why some sightings revealed map and others did not (excerpt only) ([Radiator](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/02/mapping-sea-floors-of-subnautica.html); [GOG forum](https://gog.com/forum/miasmata/please_explain_the_mapping_to_an_idiot)). **When the reveal rule is opaque, players read it as a bug.** Even admirers of Breath of the Wild's map mark it down for not tracking world changes, and Elden Ring is faulted for guidance icons that linger and for marks on one level being invisible on the other ([Stamen](https://stamen.com/cartographers-play-video-games-a-review-of-the-map-in-the-legend-of-zelda-breath-of-the-wild/); [Stamen](https://stamen.com/cartographers-play-video-games-a-review-of-the-map-in-elden-ring/)). An engine that reads doors and exits each turn can keep the map true, which those games could not.

For layers, the well-sourced devices are few and cheap. Breath of the Wild gives icons three states (undiscovered, discovered but incomplete, complete), shows place labels only when zoomed in, snaps zoom to set levels, draws the player's whole route as a "Hero's Path", and separates pins that vanish on arrival from unlabelled stamps that persist ([Stamen](https://stamen.com/cartographers-play-video-games-a-review-of-the-map-in-the-legend-of-zelda-breath-of-the-wild/)). Hollow Knight sells icon families separately and shows them only for places already visited, so the map never announces what is ahead ([SUPERJUMP](https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/getting-lost-by-design-in-hollow-knight/)). Outer Wilds keeps knowledge in a separate "detective board" view, added after playtests to help stuck players find a next step ([Mobius Digital](https://www.mobiusdigitalgames.com/news/a-look-into-playtests)). The researchers map these onto a text adventure as follows, and this mapping is inference: visited rooms as the base, untaken exits as stubs, locked or blocked ways as the third "found but unresolved" state, objects and characters shown only in visited rooms, route history as a line, and player marks as free-meaning stamps. No designer source was found on the interface for switching layers.

The closest precedent for a map as main screen is inkle's Sorcery!, a text-driven phone game. Jon Ingold calls the map "the single best design decision", the thing that "turned Sorcery! from a gamebook into a game", and lists its jobs: progress meter, visible proof of consequence, strategy layer, and checkpointing, since "you simply rewind along the line of your journey" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/postmortem-i-steve-jackson-s-sorcery-i-series-by-inkle)). Later instalments added interior maps explored room by room with prose arriving per location, the same shape as a parser game's room graph.

On who does the work, the sourced middle ground is machine-drawn geometry with player-owned meaning. Breath of the Wild's towers give terrain and the player pins what matters; Elden Ring offers a hundred markers and almost no automatic points of interest ([Stamen](https://stamen.com/cartographers-play-video-games-a-review-of-the-map-in-elden-ring/)). Geometry and room names are the tedious part an automap does reliably; "come back with a light" is the part worth leaving to the player, and it answers the players who said mapping by hand was half the pleasure. Framing the map as an object with an author also excuses its gaps: Stamen says Elden Ring's map reads as "an in-game artifact", and faults the one line set drawn in a different style. A pencil-on-paper notebook fits the medium's own tradition of boxed maps, provided every layer looks drawn by the same hand.

## Lattice placement with stretching beats re-optimising, and seen rooms should not move

Three families of layout exist for graphs whose edges carry compass directions. Text-adventure and MUD tools place each exit as a unit step on an integer grid and resolve collisions by stretching: IFM, Mudlet and nitfol all work this way, and nitfol lengthens passages by one unit, then two, then gives up, which its manual says "isn't perfect" ([IFM documentation](https://ifm.readthedocs.io/en/latest/maps.html); [nitfol manual](https://0v9xf534pt.unbox.ifarchive.org/0v9xf534pt/nitfol.html)). Metro-map methods optimise an octilinear drawing by mixed-integer programming or hill climbing and give each edge a range of three admissible directions ([Nöllenburg and Wolff](https://i11www.iti.kit.edu/extra/publications/nw-dlhqm-10.pdf); [Nickel and Nöllenburg](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1904.03039)). Constraint-based stress layout (IPSep-CoLa, WebCola) expresses "B is east of A" directly as alignment plus separation, but requires the constraints to be satisfiable ([WebCola constraints](https://github.com/tgdwyer/WebCola/wiki/Constraints)).

**No tool found handles contradictory directions automatically and well**, and contradiction is normal in these games. Mudlet users mapping without server coordinates report rooms colliding; a Trizbort user calls its automap "pretty much useless" for a game whose way back is not the opposite direction, and the remedy offered is drawing a bent line by hand ([Mudlet forum](https://forums.mudlet.org/viewtopic.php?p=12946); [intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/christminster-directional-confusion/41016)). The researchers' proposed policy is inference built on those tools: place the new room one step in the direction moved; if the cell is taken, insert an empty row or column instead of overlapping; when a loop does not close, demote the newest edge to a curved or dog-legged connector with the true exit direction shown as a stub at each end; and keep any heavier tidy pass as an explicit user action. The closest prior art, Inform 7's own spatial-map algorithm, could not be read (both mirrors refused the fetch), and Trizbort's placement code was not examined. Both are worth reading before layout work starts.

On stability, the research is narrower than folklore suggests, and it points one way. Archambault and Purchase found little effect from preserving the "mental map" on general comprehension, but a **significant benefit for orientation tasks**, finding and re-finding specific places, which is what a player's map is for ([Springer](https://rd.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-36763-2_42); [Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/the-map-in-the-mental-map-experimental-results-in-dynamic-graph-d/)). Heer and Robertson found animated transitions help people track which objects moved ([paper](https://vis.berkeley.edu/papers/animated_transitions/2007-AnimatedTransitions-InfoVis.pdf)). The design rule that follows is to never move a room the player has seen unless forced, to move the smallest set when forced, and to animate the move. Row and column insertion preserves left-of and above-of relations by construction. No source gives a threshold for how much change people tolerate.

For floors, practice agrees: one flat plan per level with a level picker (Apple's indoor maps), and vertical exits drawn as symbols on the room, as Mudlet does for up, down, in and out ([WWDC 2019](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/241); [Mudlet manual](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper)). Mudlet's separate "areas" are the standing answer to a world that will not fit one plane. A 3D view is not supported for a phone: 3D helped graph reading only with stereo and motion parallax on high-resolution displays, while Cockburn and McKenzie found performance fell as freedom in the third dimension grew ([Ware and Mitchell](https://scholars.unh.edu/ccom/467); [Cockburn and McKenzie](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/503376.503413)).

## Drawing, touch and VoiceOver: proven parts and prototype questions

The hand-drawn look is well understood. Rough.js perturbs and bows lines, fills with hachure, and takes a seed so a shape keeps the same outline between renders; a Rust port, `roughr`, exists; and Red Blob Games documents the cross-hatching used around rooms in dungeon maps ([Rough.js](https://github.com/rough-stuff/rough); [roughr](https://docs.rs/crate/roughr/latest/source/src/lib.rs); [Red Blob Games](https://redblobgames.com/x/1947-dyson-hatching)). SwiftUI's Canvas is an immediate-mode view meant for drawing many elements ([Swift with Majid](https://swiftwithmajid.com/2023/04/11/mastering-canvas-in-swiftui/)). Seeding each room's wobble by its ID, so outlines do not shimmer between frames, and drawing new rooms in by animating stroke length are the researchers' suggestions. That a few hundred rooms fit the frame budget is an expectation; no measured Canvas figures were found.

Tap-to-travel is a solved pattern: Mudlet runs A* over explored exits with weights and locks and emits a list of direction commands when a room is clicked ([Mudlet manual](https://wiki.mudlet.org/w/Manual:Mapper)). Beyond that, **phone interaction is the thinnest part of the notes.** No sourced example was gathered of a map and a text feed sharing a small screen well, and Apple's guidelines on sheets and tap targets were not fetched. The gesture set, the shrink-to-a-strip behaviour when the keyboard is up, lower-third controls, and the rule that travel should go one move per turn and stop when the game's reply lands somewhere unexpected are all inference. The last of these is well grounded in the nitfol and zMUD failure lists: the map cannot know about a wandering thief or a state-changing exit until it tries. Breath of the Wild's division between a near view for wayfinding and a far view for planning, with snapped zoom between them, is the sourced model for two zoom states of one map ([Stamen](https://stamen.com/cartographers-play-video-games-a-review-of-the-map-in-the-legend-of-zelda-breath-of-the-wild/)).

Accessibility has one hard fact and a clear research direction. A Canvas exposes no individual elements to VoiceOver; a parallel hierarchy must be supplied with `accessibilityChildren` ([WWDC21 notes](https://mackuba.eu/notes/wwdc21/add-rich-graphics-to-swiftui-app)). Research on accessible node-link diagrams converges on giving an overview first, making each node a focusable element that speaks its name and connections, and letting navigation follow the diagram's own structure; TADA evaluated this with 25 blind and low-vision participants, and Benthic argues navigation should mirror the visual structure ([TADA](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.04502v4); [Benthic](https://vis.csail.mit.edu/pubs/benthic)). Apple's audio-graph API describes data series and does not fit a room graph ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/accessibility/audio-graphs)). The proposed shape is inference: each room an element with its exits as actions, current room first in swipe order, and a plain searchable list of visited places as the fallback that always works. That list has precedent in the games themselves, since Curses has a "places" command and Short recommends GO TO by name ([Emily Short, Geography](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/geography/)). The app already speaks each game reply to a screen reader, so map announcements must share that channel. Nothing was found on blind players' use of maps in text adventures or MUDs, and whether touch exploration works over a panned and zoomed canvas needs a prototype.

## Conclusion

The notes change where the difficulty sits. The community's long-standing verdict that interpreters cannot map was a verdict about identifying rooms from text, and this app does not have that problem. Its problems are the ones the commercial maps and MUD clients quietly designed around: fitting an inconsistent world on a page, and deciding how much truth to show. The first is best met by not attempting a single consistent plane at all, using a local view, separate sheets and honest bent connectors, with a correction tool as part of the design from the start. The second is new. Because the engine sees exits the player has not noticed, objects in every room and the true wiring of every maze, the map can spoil more thoroughly than any InvisiClues sheet, and the reveal rule (what counts as "the player has found this") becomes the central design decision, to be stated plainly to the player so it never reads as a bug.

The same access is the opportunity none of the prior art had. Suspended's counters, Deadline's cast, a door that opens, a wall the Cyclops breaks: an engine that reads the object tree each turn can keep the map true to the world, which is the flaw even the most admired video-game maps are marked down for. Three checks would convert the largest unknowns into facts before design is fixed: read Inform 7's spatial-map source and Trizbort's placement code, confirm the unverified set pieces against the corpus story files, and prototype VoiceOver exploration and the map-with-keyboard layout on a device.
